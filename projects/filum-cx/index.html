Filum CX is Filum AI’s main product. Businesses use it to talk to their customers on every channel from one inbox, with AI agents and automations handling the repetitive work.

I worked on it for two years in a 15-person engineering team. I designed and led the reliability layer for the integrations, owned the automation work, and added features to conversations and tickets. This page covers the problems I took on and what changed because of them.

Making channel integrations reliable

Filum CX supports more than 80 integrations, running on Cloud Functions. When a channel integration broke, the customer was the first to notice. They told our customer service team, CS checked and passed it to the developers, the developers traced the problem, and then the answer travelled back to the customer.

I designed the system that changed this for more than 10 channels, and built most of it.

Kind of failureBeforeAfter
Expired credential (token, login)Found by the customer. 2–3 hours of back-and-forth for an experienced developer, about half a day for a newer one, then the customer re-authorizesThe customer sees the connection is about to expire, or has expired, and re-authorizes it themselves, before anything stops working
Infrastructure (Cloud Function out of memory, traffic spike, channel API errors)Half a day to a full day to find the cause, then the missed data had to be re-run by hand for the customerThe cause is visible right away in centralized logs. Failed jobs retry automatically, and if a retry still fails, the team gets an alert in its internal channel
  1. Credential monitoringTracks the state of every connection and shows customers when one is close to expiring, so they can re-authorize it in time.
  2. Centralized loggingLogs from all integrations in one place, so a failure points straight at its cause instead of a long trace.
  3. Automatic retriesData that failed to sync is processed again without anyone re-running it.
  4. Internal alertsWhen retries are not enough, the team is told first, often before the customer would notice.
Result: support moved from “the customer reports a bug” to “the team already knows, and customers can fix the most common issue themselves”. I also cut monthly logging costs by 60%, from $500 to $200, by removing redundant logs and tuning Cloud Run resources.

The hardest bug: a view count that was always wrong

The conversation view count, the number of conversations in each view of the inbox, had been wrong for as long as anyone could remember. It got fixed again and again, and a few days later a customer would report it was off again. Nobody had found the root cause.

I traced it to how the count was built: it was calculated on the frontend, with no source of truth from the backend, so it drifted as conversations changed. Patching the frontend could never hold.

I added a flow where the backend sends a snapshot of the counts to the frontend every time something changes. Customers stopped reporting wrong counts after that.

Conversations, tickets, and speed

SLA and attributes

Added SLAs and custom attributes to conversations and tickets, so support teams see what is about to breach and can classify requests.

15 s to about 8 s

Reordered the API calls: what loads first, what can wait, and what is not called until the user opens that part. Page load for customers dropped from 15 seconds to just over 8.

Lighter frontend

Lazy loading, tree shaking, list virtualization, and render optimization to cut wasted work in the browser.
